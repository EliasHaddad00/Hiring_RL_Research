# Bracket the hiring optimum, then test PPO

The plan's central move is to build the yardsticks before the agents: an exact dynamic-programming optimum on a small instance, a rigorous upper bound on the full problem and a stronger theory-derived rule, all in place by 25 November, so that every later PPO number is read against a known ceiling. The research notes' prototypes, which have not yet been reproduced in the team's simulator, put that ceiling at about **4.17** in the decided parameter set at h0 = 0.02, against **3.54** for the team's best one-interview rule and **3.72** for a prototype price-directed rule. The optimum therefore sits at most about 18% above today's best rule and about 12% above the prototype rule, which makes "PPO ties the best rule" the most likely outcome and a clear PPO win the surprise. The plan treats that tie as a planned result: it pre-registers a 2% equivalence margin, fixes seed pools and tuning budgets before the final runs, and maps each of the instructor's four negative-result conditions to a named figure, table or test. Three of the four research questions (how close rules get to the optimum, where index structure breaks, whether the belief correction changes decisions) return reportable findings however PPO training goes. Compute is not the binding constraint (a toy-environment measurement suggests the whole campaign fits in tens of core-hours); people-time and the five-page limit are, so work is organised into five workstreams with four go/no-go gates on 18 November, 25 November, 2 December and 9 December and an ordered cut list. The project is finished when every research question has a verdict from its pre-specified decision rule, the repository regenerates every figure from a clean clone with one command, and the five-page ICML-format paper is tagged to that commit.

*Labels used throughout.* "Prototype" means a number computed by the note-takers in their own short scripts or on a toy environment, not in the team's simulator. "Notes' inference" means the note-takers' own derivation or recommendation, not a published result. "Unverified" and "secondary source" are carried over from the notes as written. "Planning default" marks a choice this plan makes where the notes give a range or no value; each is listed again under open items.

## Done means four verdicts and a reproducible repository

The proposal said what the project is. This plan says what gets built, why, what the team expects to see, and the test that ends each piece of work. Its purpose is to make the 40% Completion grade a matter of ticking pre-agreed tests, and to make sure the 60% for Contribution, Novelty and Writing does not depend on PPO beating a rule.

That last point is the main design consequence of the notes. The team's preliminary simulation already shows that the decided regime is not rich in hard decisions: a second interview was never used and the three-per-day interview limit never bound. The note-takers' relaxation agrees: with the role cap and the interview cap priced, the optimal interview price was zero and the relaxed policy used about 14 of 180 interview slots (prototype). A task with a slack capacity constraint and a ceiling within roughly a tenth of a good rule leaves little room for a learned policy. A hypothesis-driven study of why that is so is more defensible on a CPU budget than a ranking claim, which is the position the 2024 benchmarking critique takes when it recommends "scientific testing" over leaderboard comparisons ([Jordan et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16241)).

The definition of done has seven parts, and each maps to a grading criterion.

## Four questions, each with a hypothesis the data can refute

The notes change what the team should expect on every question. On RQ1 they supply the ceiling. On RQ2 they show headroom is narrow. On RQ3 they show that theory already predicts index failure under departures, so the finding is the size and location of the gap, and that a closely related hiring-motivated model found index-type policies near 100% of optimum on average despite proven non-indexability ([Aouad, Ji, Shaposhnik](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508); figure read through a summarising fetch and to be re-checked before quoting). On RQ4 they show the correction is a standard frailty calculation that a network given candidate age can learn implicitly, so the measurable effect is on rule decisions and on sample efficiency.

Steelmanning the alternatives matters here. A gap between the two learned policies can come from optimisation differences rather than expressiveness, and the unrestricted flat network differs from the index network in two ways at once: it sees the whole pool, and it lacks weight sharing across candidate slots. The plan addresses the first with matched tuning budgets, an identical critic and the exact small instance, and the second with a pooled (Deep Sets style) policy that keeps the symmetry and adds only pool awareness.

## Eight components, each with a reason and an acceptance test

### The environment is a single Gymnasium class with one mask and fixed scaling

The simulator is wrapped as a plain `gym.Env` with an `action_masks()` method, a `Discrete(2N+1)` action space (candidate index and interview-or-offer, plus "end day"), and a fixed top-N candidate observation. The verified stack as of 12 November 2026 is Gymnasium 1.4.0, Stable-Baselines3 2.9.2 and SB3-Contrib 2.9.2, which installed and trained together on Python 3.13 in the note-takers' sandbox; SB3 2.9.2 is the last release to support Python 3.10 and the maintainers recommend 3.11 or later ([SB3 changelog](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/misc/changelog.html), [Gymnasium release notes](https://gymnasium.farama.org/gymnasium_release_notes/)). Five implementation rules follow from the notes and each prevents a silent failure.

Day 60 returns `terminated=True`, not `truncated=True`, because the deadline is part of the task and "days left" is observed; with truncation SB3 would bootstrap from a state that has no future ([Pardo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.00378), abstract only read). Observations are fresh arrays, because the 1.4.0 checker now requires distinct observation objects per step ([Gymnasium release notes](https://gymnasium.farama.org/gymnasium_release_notes/)). Scaling is fixed and identical for every candidate slot inside the environment, never `VecNormalize` on observations, because per-dimension running statistics would scale slot 3 differently from slot 7 and break weight sharing in the index policy (notes' inference). "End day" is always valid and the environment asserts `mask.any()`, because an all-false mask raised no error in the local test and produced a uniform distribution over all actions, invalid ones included. Masks are always passed at evaluation, through the maskable `evaluate_policy` and `MaskableEvalCallback`, because omitting them is silent ([MaskablePPO docs](https://sb3-contrib.readthedocs.io/en/master/modules/ppo_mask.html)). The flat `Discrete(2N+1)` head with one joint mask is kept; a `MultiDiscrete([N, 2])` head would be masked per dimension and could not express "interview is invalid for candidate 4 only", the failure pattern documented for per-sub-action masks ([Bamford and Ovalle 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.07294)).

Planning defaults for the observation are N = 10 slots (21 actions); per candidate a presence flag, belief mean, belief spread or interview count, days since arrival and an interviewed-today flag; globally days left, roles left, interview slots left today and pool size. Slot ordering (stable slot per candidate versus sorted by belief mean) is chosen once, kept identical across all policy variants and reported; the environment logs how often the pool exceeds N. Each episode seed fixes arrival times, hidden qualities, interview noise and the uniform draws for departures and acceptances, indexed by candidate and not by call order, so that every policy faces the same world and comparisons are paired (notes' inference). The same class also runs the exact small instance through a configuration switch, so a pass there exonerates the code path used on the full problem.

Three conventions change the formulas in the dynamic program and the bound and must be pinned in week 1: at most one interview per candidate per day, offers resolved one at a time within the offer phase, and a candidate interviewed today can be offered tomorrow at the earliest. The environment exists because every other component reads it; it is accepted when `check_env` passes on a variant that tolerates invalid actions (the checker samples actions without the mask), a random masked rollout never executes an invalid action, identical seeds give identical trajectories, empirical arrival rate, acceptance curve and departure hazard match their configured values, the team's preliminary rule scores (3.10 for the fixed threshold; 3.93, 3.71, 3.54, 3.42, 3.14 for the one-interview rule) reproduce within 0.05, and throughput has been read from `time/fps` on a 100,000-step run.

### Two parameter sets separate a calibrated market from a stress test

The notes compared the decided parameters with hiring statistics. Horizon, arrival rate and interview cap are supported; acceptance decay and the upper hazard are not. Median time to fill is about a month and a half overall and 60 to 61 days at employers with 5,000 or more staff ([SHRM 2025](https://www.shrm.org/content/dam/en/shrm/research/2025-recruiting-benchmarking-report.pdf)), and median time to first fill is 60 days for business roles and 75 for technical roles ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/recruiting-operations-benchmarks-talent-trends)), so T = 60 is realistic and moderately tight for three roles. About 200 applications per hire with 6% of inbound applicants passing the first screen ([Gem 2025](https://lp.gem.com/rs/972-IVV-330/images/2025%20Recruiting%20Benchmarks%20-%20Gem.pdf?version=0)) implies 0.2 to 0.6 screened-in candidates per role per day, so 1.5 arrivals per day across three roles fits only if an arrival is defined as a candidate who has passed résumé screening (notes' inference). Twenty interviews per hire in 2024 ([Gem 2025](https://lp.gem.com/rs/972-IVV-330/images/2025%20Recruiting%20Benchmarks%20-%20Gem.pdf?version=0)) averages one interview a day for three hires, so a cap of three is slack on average.

Offer acceptance is 76 to 84% in applicant-tracking data ([Ashby](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/2023-trends-report-offer-acceptance-rates), [Gem 2025](https://lp.gem.com/rs/972-IVV-330/images/2025%20Recruiting%20Benchmarks%20-%20Gem.pdf?version=0)). With the decided curve, acceptance is 0.73 at day 7 and 0.37 at day 30, so matching 80% requires offers within about four days; if offers typically come five to six weeks after application, the implied decay is 0.002 to 0.0035 per day, about a tenth of 0.03 (notes' inference). For the hazard, stated disengagement of 12% at one week and 36% at one month ([Cronofy 2024](https://www.cronofy.com/reports/candidate-expectations-report-2024)) implies h0 of roughly 0.012 to 0.017, which the notes call weak evidence; h0 = 0.10 implies a top-decile hazard of 0.29 per day, harsher than even the folk claim that top candidates are gone in ten days, which traces to an unsupported assertion and must not be cited as data ([Sullivan, republished 2020](https://ca.drakeintl.com/drakepulse/the-top-12-reasons-why-slow-hiring-severely-damages-recruiting-and-business-results)). Interview validity of r = .42 for structured interviews maps to a noise standard deviation of 2.16 and biodata at r = .38 to 2.43 ([summary of Sackett et al.](https://master-hr.com/insights/new-study-providing-updated-validity-estimates/), secondary source), so the decided interview noise of 1.0 makes an interview far more informative than the evidence supports.

| Parameter | Stress scenario (the decided set) | Calibrated scenario | Status of the calibrated value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizon, roles, arrivals, interview cap | 60 days, 3 roles, 1.5 per day, 3 per day | Same; arrival defined as screened-in candidate | Supported by cited benchmarks |
| Offer acceptance | 0.9 × exp(−0.03 d) | 0.9 × exp(−0.003 d) | Notes' inference from acceptance rates; no source gives acceptance by day |
| Departure hazard h0 | 0, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10 | 0.02 | Weak evidence; range 0.01 to 0.05 |
| Interview noise SD | 1.0 | 2.2 | Secondary source |
| Résumé signal SD | 1.5 | 2.4 (planning default; the notes say "2.4 or more") | Secondary source; open item |
| Quality slope 0.6, exponential decay form, interview cost 0.05 | As decided | As decided | No empirical basis found for any of the three |

The stress scenario stays the primary test bed because the project was built on it and the preliminary numbers exist there. The calibrated scenario exists for two reasons: it answers the "is this realistic?" question with labelled evidence, and it is a hurdle experiment in its own right, because if time pressure is much weaker there, simple threshold rules will be near-optimal and that is a task-structure explanation for RL adding nothing (notes' inference). A calculation made for this report with the notes' one-step formula gives an interview zone half-width of about 0.25 before the first interview and 0.19 before the second in the calibrated scenario, against 0.50 and 0.24 in the stress scenario, so the interview decision survives calibration and second interviews become relatively more attractive; this is unverified until the simulator confirms it. Calibration is accepted when both configurations are frozen in files, a compact mapping table with sources is in the repository, and three sanity statistics are reported for the best rule in each scenario: share of roles filled by day 60 (the constant-hazard reading of vacancy data implies 84 to 93% of single vacancies fill within 60 days; [Davis, Faberman, Haltiwanger](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16265/revisions/w16265.rev0.pdf) and notes' inference), realised acceptance rate against 76 to 84%, and interviews per hire against 20. The prototype price-directed rule used about 14 interviews per episode, under five per hire, so the simulator will be interview-light relative to the benchmark and the paper should say so.

### The belief module is exact, small and shared by every other component

Conditional on a candidate's signals and on still being in the pool after d poaching rounds, the posterior over quality is the usual Gaussian posterior multiplied by the survival factor (1 − h(q))^d, which depends only on d. The exact sufficient statistic is therefore (naive mean, number of interviews, days waited), and no belief grid is needed beyond a quadrature for expectations (notes' inference). The module returns the corrected mean, the probability of surviving tonight and the predictive distribution of the next interview result, and exposes the naive mean alongside for the ablation. A first-order approximation, mean − 0.6 · d · h0 · σ² · exp(0.6 · mean + 0.18 · σ²), is provided for hand-written rules only; it matched quadrature at small d · h0 (1.305 against 1.312) and failed at large d · h0 (0.525 against 0.806) in the prototype.

It exists because four components depend on it: the survival-aware agent and rules (RQ4), the exact small instance, the Lagrangian bound and the optional lower-variance training reward. The statistical idea is not new. Time on the market as a rational negative signal is established in labour economics as dynamic selection ([Jarosch and Pilossoph](https://nber.org/papers/w24200)) and in recruiting theory, where talented workers are removed from the pool faster ([Board, Meyer-ter-Vehn, Sadzik](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/sboard/papers/talent.pdf)); the hazard is a proportional-hazards model with a log-normal frailty term (notes' inference). The module is accepted when quadrature reproduces the two check values above, a Monte Carlo calibration test passes (among simulated survivors grouped by naive mean and age, average true quality equals the corrected mean within sampling error), and the team's figures of 0.87, 0.71 and 0.47 are reproduced. A check made for this report at the résumé-only variance of 0.692 and seven rounds gave 0.88, 0.72 and 0.48; the small residual should be reconciled in week 1.

### Rule baselines come from theory first and tuning second

The decided rule set is a fixed threshold, a falling-bar threshold, a one-interview rule and a Weitzman-style index rule. The notes give each a theoretical anchor, which matters for the Contribution grade because a tuned rule with a derivation is a stronger baseline than a tuned rule alone. Every rule here is a heuristic adaptation: no paper found treats partial inspection, departures, a deadline and more than one hire together.

The falling bar is anchored by finite-horizon stopping theory: with a known distribution the optimal bar equals the marginal value of a slot, falls as the deadline nears and is lower when more roles are open ([Ferguson 1989](https://www.or.mist.i.u-tokyo.ac.jp/takeda/FreshmanCourse/Ferguson.pdf) for the single-choice recursion; the multi-slot recursion is the notes' derivation). For the stress parameters with about 90 arrivals left and no interviews, the résumé-estimate bars are roughly 0.98, 1.07 and 1.22 for three, two and one open roles, and the no-interview optimum with no recall is about 3.28 (prototype). The one-interview rule is anchored by Weitzman's reservation value, the z solving E[(X − z)⁺] = c ([as restated by Qin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13775v2)), which for a Gaussian is mean + s · Ψ⁻¹(c/s). Treating one interview as a partial inspection gives a three-zone rule around the bar: fast-track above the zone, interview inside it, pass below it, with half-widths 0.50, 0.24 and 0.13 before the first, second and third interview (notes' inference, following the continuation-region structure in [Chick and Frazier 2009](https://www.informs-sim.org/wsc09papers/051.pdf)). That reproduces the team's fast-track rule from first principles and predicts the observed absence of second interviews. Fast-tracking is "taking the box closed", the optional-inspection case that no simple ranking rule solves optimally ([Beyhaghi and Kleinberg](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.01428)).

The Weitzman-style index rule should be implemented as the price-directed rule from the Lagrangian relaxation, because one one-candidate dynamic program then yields the exact per-candidate index, the baseline policy and the upper bound. Each day the rule recomputes the price per hire and follows each candidate's one-candidate action at that price. In the prototype it scored **3.91, 3.72 and 3.52** at h0 = 0, 0.02 and 0.05 (3,000 episodes, ±0.03), against 3.71, 3.54 and 3.42 for the team's best one-interview rule at the corresponding pressure levels. The two sets come from different simulators, so the 0.1 to 0.2 difference is a lead, not a result; if it reproduces, the "best rule" that PPO must beat moves up. A random valid policy is added as the normalisation anchor. Two theory-constant rules with no tuning (a balanced threshold of 1.09 and a half-of-expected-maximum threshold of 0.80, both prototype translations of the deadline papers) are optional rows for the repository; the notes expect them to under-perform.

Each rule is run with the naive and the survival-aware belief, fitted on the tuning episode pool with the same number of configurations as PPO receives, and reported on the held-out pool. Rules exist to answer RQ1, to set the bar for RQ2 and to carry the clean test of RQ4. They are accepted when the h0 = 0 shape facts hold in the simulator (bar lower with more open roles, bar at zero on the last day), the tuned falling bar is consistent with the stopping-theory values, and the price-directed rule either reproduces the prototype scores within their intervals or the discrepancy is traced to a simulator convention.

### An exact small instance turns "PPO loses by x" into a diagnosis

The recommended instance has one role, 12 days, an arrival with probability 0.6 each day, one interview slot, a pool cap of two, cost 0.05, h0 = 0.05, the full model's acceptance curve, and binned observations: three résumé bins with cut points at −0.8 and 0.8, three interview bins with cut points at 0 and 1, and at most two interviews per candidate. Hidden quality stays standard normal; only the observation channel is coarsened, which makes the state space finite and the solution exact up to quadrature error. Memoised backward induction over (day, pool) solved it in **3.1 seconds with 165,558 start-of-day states and an optimal value of 0.6335**, and an independent Monte Carlo run of the extracted policy gave 0.631 ± 0.004 over 40,000 episodes (prototype, the note-taker's own design; no published instance exists to cross-check). Raising the pool cap to three changed the optimum by 0.1% at 185 times the run time at T = 10, so cap two stands.

The instance exists for three reasons. It gives RQ1 a true denominator. It is the positive control for PPO: if PPO reaches the optimum here and not on the full problem, the code is exonerated and the shortfall is a scaling or credit-assignment issue (notes' inference). And it prices mistakes exactly, because the dynamic program supplies the value of every action in every state. Policies are scored by exact policy evaluation (the same recursion with the policy's action in place of the maximum) where feasible, and otherwise by 40,000-episode Monte Carlo. Its limit is stated up front: with one role, one slot and a cap of two it tests fast-track versus interview, the second interview, holding versus offering and the falling bar, and it does not test slot allocation or the three-role slot-value effect. It is accepted when the team's port reproduces 0.5838, 0.6335 and 0.6947 at T = 10, 12 and 15, the extracted policy's simulated return matches the computed value within two standard errors in the team's own environment, and the recursions have been checked against the simulator's offer and interview conventions.

### A Lagrangian bound caps the full problem at about 4.2

Two bounds are computed. The hindsight bound reveals everything and equals the expected sum of the top three positive qualities among arrivals who would accept on day 0; it is 6.34 for three roles, does not depend on h0, cost or noise, and is about 70% above the best constructed policy (prototype). It is kept as a one-hour sanity line and as a demonstration that perfect-information bounds without penalties are weak, which the information-relaxation literature states directly ([Brown, Smith, Sun 2010](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Information_Relaxations_and_Duality_in_Stochastic_Dynamic_Programs.pdf)).

The working bound relaxes "at most m hires" with a price per hire and "at most K interviews per day" with a price per interview, which decouples candidates into one-candidate dynamic programs; this is the Lagrangian approach for dynamic selection problems ([Brown and Smith 2020](https://jimsmith.host.dartmouth.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Dynamic-Selection-Problems.pdf)) applied to this model by the note-taker. It ran in about 25 seconds per hazard level in numpy and gave **4.31, 4.24, 4.17 and 4.00** for three roles at h0 = 0, 0.01, 0.02 and 0.05 (prototype; grid and quadrature error not rigorously controlled). It did not change when the per-candidate interview cap rose from three to six. Its single-hire special case has the same form as the published deadline bound OPT ≤ min over t of [t + W(t)] ([Qin et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13775v2)). A tighter penalty-based information-relaxation bound is guaranteed to be no worse, and the notes judge it more than a week of student effort; it is out of scope.

The bound exists so that the full problem has a ceiling where no exact optimum is possible, and so the paper can report both "percent of bound" and "percent of the best rule" for every policy. It is accepted when it has been regenerated for every setting in the experiment grid (the notes did not compute h0 = 0.10 or the calibrated scenario), a finer grid changes it by less than 0.01, it is at least as large as every evaluated policy at every setting, and the small-instance analogue is at least the exact optimum. A violation of either inequality is a bug in the bound or the simulator and blocks the gate.

### Two agents share a critic and differ only in the actor

The unrestricted agent is `MaskablePPO("MlpPolicy")` with separate 64 × 64 actor and critic networks and needs no custom code. The index-structured agent subclasses `MaskableActorCriticPolicy`, overrides `_build_mlp_extractor` with a module that applies one shared network to each candidate's features plus the global features and emits the 2N + 1 logits directly, and replaces SB3's `action_net` with `nn.Identity()`. Leaving the default dense output layer in place is the one thing that silently destroys the index structure, because it mixes all candidates (notes' inference from the [policy source](https://sb3-contrib.readthedocs.io/en/v2.7.0/_modules/sb3_contrib/common/maskable/policies.html) and the [custom-policy guide](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/custom_policy.html)). The note-taker's implementation trained on a toy environment, passed a permutation-equivariance check and round-tripped through save and load, at 12,868 parameters and about 3,900 steps per second; it has not been run on the team's simulator. The critic is the same unrestricted network in both agents so that only the actor differs; the critic is not used at deployment, and the paper states this choice.

Two restrictions need words in the paper. The index network sees the global clock and roles left, so it is a time- and state-dependent priority rule, richer than Weitzman's static reservation value; the paper should call it an "index-structured (separable per-candidate priority) policy" and keep the computed price-directed rule as the classical index (notes' inference). And the end-day logit is computed from global features only, which is a real modelling restriction to state and ablate.

The starting configuration below is the notes' synthesis from library defaults and two tuning studies; it is not validated on the team's simulator. Discounting is the parameter most likely to matter: with 100 to 300 agent steps per episode, 0.99²⁰⁰ is 0.13 while 0.999²⁰⁰ is 0.82, the objective is undiscounted, and the discount factor is among the most important PPO choices ([Andrychowicz et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05990v1)).

`DummyVecEnv` ran two to three times faster than `SubprocVecEnv` on a cheap numpy environment in the local test, so runs are parallelised across cores, one single-threaded process per run. Every run writes SB3's `progress.csv`, which already holds return, value loss, entropy loss, approximate KL, clip fraction and explained variance ([SB3 logger docs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/common/logger.html)). A custom callback adds entropy divided by the log of the number of valid actions, because maximum entropy changes with pool size; in the toy test entropy started near 1.47 with 21 actions, far from log 21 = 3.04, only because few actions were valid. A maskable evaluation callback on a fixed-seed environment supplies the headline curve in raw, unshaped return.

The agents are accepted when the equivariance, finite-logit and masked-probability unit tests pass; the same code path reproduces a known score on a standard task such as CartPole; PPO beats the rules within minutes on a tiny overfit instance (three slots, short horizon); the degenerate regimes behave as expected (zero interview cost, zero interview noise, no pressure, very high hazard); and at least one architecture reaches the exact small-instance optimum within 2%.

### Ablations are chosen to separate mechanisms

Each ablation exists to rule out one explanation, and the list is fixed before the final runs. The must-run set is the four one-at-a-time sweeps on discount, entropy coefficient, learning rate and rollout size (three seeds each, which double as the tuning-sensitivity evidence); the belief arm (naive versus survival-aware, with age observed in both); the pooled Deep Sets style policy, which adds a masked mean of candidate embeddings to each candidate's input and so separates pool awareness from symmetry; potential-based reward shaping on versus off; and the end-day logit computed from globals versus from a pooled summary. Shaping uses a potential equal to a simple estimate of the pool's value, with the potential at zero in the terminal state and the same discount as PPO, which is the form that preserves optimal policies ([course slides summarising Ng, Harada and Russell](https://teach.cs.toronto.edu/~csc2542h/fall/material/csc2542f16_reward_shaping.pdf), secondary source); unshaped return is always what is reported.

The optional set, run only if gate 3 is met on time, is masking versus a small invalid-action penalty on a small N (the published comparison found penalties do not scale; [Huang and Ontañón](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.14171v3)), network width, N = 20, paying the posterior mean at acceptance in place of realised quality as a lower-variance training reward (valid only with the correct belief; notes' inference), a hazard-misspecification test in which the agent's belief uses the wrong h0, and a capacity-stress cell with one interview per day. The Deep Sets variant was not tested locally and its pitfalls are known: pooling must multiply by the presence mask, mean pooling must divide by the count of present candidates with a clamp for an empty pool, and the count must be passed as a feature because mean pooling hides it (notes' inference). An attention variant is out of scope.

## The protocol fixes seeds, budgets and statistics before the final runs

The evaluation literature converges on four rules: report intervals, never tune and evaluate on the same seeds, give every method the same tuning budget, and publish every hyperparameter and per-run result ([Agarwal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264), [Patterson et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01315), [Henderson et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.06560)). The protocol below is the notes' synthesis with planning defaults filled in.

**Seed pools.** Three disjoint pools are written into a config file before any run: tuning training seeds (three per configuration), final training seeds (15 per condition), and episode seeds split into a 4,000-episode tuning pool and a 10,000-episode evaluation pool. No choice is made on the final or evaluation pools. Re-evaluating the selected configuration on new seeds is the standard fix for the upward bias of picking the best of many noisy configurations ([Patterson et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01315)); rule thresholds are fitted parameters and get the same treatment.

**Evaluation episodes.** Every policy is evaluated on the same 10,000 episode seeds. With a return standard deviation of about 1.3 and a mean near 3.5 (the team's 0.02 standard error at 4,000 episodes is consistent with this), 10,000 episodes give a 95% half-width of 0.025, or 0.7% of the mean. Detecting a 2% difference unpaired needs about 5,400 episodes per policy at 80% power, and pairing on common random numbers cuts that in proportion to one minus the correlation, which is measured and reported (notes' calculation).

**Unit of analysis.** Episode noise answers whether one trained policy beats a rule; training-seed variance answers whether PPO as a procedure does. The second is the paper's claim, so the unit is the training seed, each seed's score is its 10,000-episode mean, and the bootstrap runs over seeds (notes' inference). Fifteen seeds per condition is the working number; ten is the floor, the smallest count for which percentile bootstrap intervals are reported to behave well ([Agarwal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264)). Because the bootstrap test is unreliable below 20 samples, Welch's t-test is reported alongside it and the family of primary comparisons is corrected for multiplicity ([Colas et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08295)).

**Tuning budgets.** Each rule and each PPO architecture gets 40 configurations (planning default). PPO configurations are scored as the mean of three tuning seeds at a reduced step budget, searched with a hand-written Optuna objective, because RL Zoo's sampler table has no `ppo_mask` entry ([hyperparams_opt.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DLR-RM/rl-baselines3-zoo/master/rl_zoo3/hyperparams_opt.py)); ranges are the table above, and any range whose best value sits on its boundary is widened. Tuning happens at the primary setting and on the small instance, the frozen configuration is applied everywhere, and that is stated as a limitation. In a paper that may report a negative result the conservative direction is to give PPO at least as much tuning as the rules and to say so (notes' inference).

**Settings and runs.** Each simulator setting is treated as a task. The primary setting is the stress scenario at h0 = 0.05 (planning default: it matches the small instance's hazard, sits mid-range and has prototype bound and rule numbers).

| Setting | Role | Rules, both beliefs | Bound | PPO conditions (15 seeds each) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exact small instance | Positive control, RQ1 denominator | Yes | Exact optimum | Index, unrestricted |
| Stress, no decay and h0 = 0 | Known-answer regime | Yes | Yes | Five seeds per architecture |
| Stress, h0 = 0 (decay only) | RQ3 baseline | Yes | Yes | Index, unrestricted, pooled |
| Stress, h0 = 0.02 | RQ2 to RQ4 | Yes | Yes | Index and unrestricted × two beliefs; pooled |
| Stress, h0 = 0.05 (primary) | RQ2 to RQ4, tuning, learning-curve figure | Yes | Yes | Index and unrestricted × two beliefs; pooled |
| Stress, h0 = 0.10 | RQ3 and RQ4 extreme | Yes | Yes | Index and unrestricted × two beliefs; pooled |
| Calibrated | Realism check, hurdle experiment | Yes | Yes | Index and unrestricted × two beliefs |
| Stress, h0 = 0.05, one interview per day | Exploratory capacity stress | Yes | Yes | Optional |

That is about 370 confirmatory runs. At a planning figure of 3 million steps per run and the toy-environment throughput of 3,900 to 7,000 steps per second, the confirmatory grid costs roughly 45 to 80 core-hours and tuning another 10 to 20, about a day of wall-clock on eight cores. No source says how many steps this task needs, and the throughput is a toy measurement, so both numbers are replaced in week 1 and at gate 2; training length is then set to at least twice the observed plateau point.

**Statistics.** Returns are normalised as (J − J_random) / (J_reference − J_random), with the exact optimum as reference on the small instance and the best tuned rule elsewhere. The paper reports the interquartile mean with a stratified bootstrap 95% interval across settings, a performance profile, the probability of improvement of PPO over the best rule, and the optimality gap on the small instance, the set recommended for few-run deep RL ([Agarwal et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.13264)). The headline number for each comparison is the 95% interval on the difference, not a check of interval overlap, which is a low-powered test ([Patterson et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01315)). Learning curves show 95% intervals, not standard errors. The `rliable` package implements these aggregates; its function names were not re-checked in the notes.

**Pre-registration.** A one-page `PREREGISTRATION.md` is committed and tagged before the final seeds run, and its commit hash is cited in the paper. It holds the hypotheses, the primary metric and comparison, the decision rule and margin, the seed lists, tuning budgets, training length, the planned ablations and known-answer tests, the rule that no seed is dropped, and an empty deviations section; anything not in it is labelled exploratory. Machine learning has piloted this as a workshop format in which protocols are reviewed before results exist ([NeurIPS 2020 pre-registration workshop](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2020/workshop/16158)); there is no binding standard, and the notes estimate the file at about an hour of work.

**Display items.** Five double-column pages hold about four display items plus roughly 3,000 words (notes' estimate, not a sourced figure). The paper carries one full-width three-panel learning-curve figure at the primary setting (evaluation return with random, best-rule and bound reference lines; policy and value loss; normalised entropy), one main results table (methods by settings with intervals, plus percent of bound and percent of exact optimum), one single-column figure of the unrestricted-minus-index gap against h0, one single-column figure for the belief effect or the failure-mode breakdown depending on the outcome, and one compact tuning-and-ablation table. Per-seed curves, approximate KL, clip fraction, explained variance, the calibration table and all known-answer outputs live in the repository and are referenced by path.

## Every outcome has a pre-written reading

The primary decision rule is applied to the 95% interval of the mean paired difference, with a margin of 2% of the comparator's return (about 0.07 at a return of 3.5): entirely inside the margin means equivalent; otherwise entirely above zero means better; entirely below zero means worse; anything else is inconclusive (notes' recommendation; the margin is the team's choice and no source prescribes one). Equivalence takes precedence, so a difference that is detectable and inside the margin is reported as practically negligible.

| Question and outcome | What the paper claims | What it triggers |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1, rules at 95% or more of the exact optimum and within about 10% of the bound | The task's structure makes simple rules near-optimal; state how much headroom any learner has | Headroom becomes the first "fundamental hurdle" |
| RQ1, rules below 95% on the small instance | There is exploitable structure that rules miss; name the states where the optimal policy differs | Those states become the test set for PPO |
| RQ2 small instance, PPO within 2% of optimum | The implementation and observation design are sound | Full-problem results are interpreted as scaling behaviour |
| RQ2 small instance, PPO outside 2% | PPO does not solve even the small problem with this budget | Imitation test: if a cloned network matches the optimal policy, the failure is optimisation or exploration; if it cannot, the observation or architecture is insufficient. Warm start: if return degrades from the cloned policy, the gradient signal is the problem |
| RQ2 full problem, PPO better | PPO finds value the rules miss; report the share of the rule-to-bound gap it closes | Explain where: state-level comparison with the best rule on shared episodes |
| RQ2 full problem, equivalent | PPO matches the best rule to within 2%; with the bound, state the maximum either could still gain | Negative-result package |
| RQ2 full problem, worse or inconclusive | PPO does not reach the best rule under a stated budget | Negative-result package; "inconclusive" is reported as such with the interval |
| RQ3, gap inside the margin at every h0 | Index structure survives departures in this regime, consistent with near-optimal index-type policies in a related non-indexable model | Report the capacity-stress cell as the place to look next |
| RQ3, gap grows with h0 | Size and location of the loss from index structure, which theory predicts in sign but not magnitude | Confirm on the small instance by imitation of the exact policy; confirm with the pooled variant that pool awareness is the cause |

| RQ4, PPO arms equivalent in final return | A network given candidate age learns the correction implicitly; the benefit is in learning speed or interpretability | Report steps-to-threshold; do not claim an asymptotic gain |

## Conclusion

The notes shift the project's centre of gravity from the agent to the yardsticks. With an exact optimum, a rigorous ceiling and a theory-derived rule in hand, the paper's most likely headline is a measured statement about the task: how much value a hiring race of this shape leaves on the table for any policy beyond a good rule, and which features (hazard, capacity, signal noise) open that gap. PPO then serves as the instrument that probes the gap, and the index-versus-unrestricted contrast and the belief correction become two controlled probes of where structure matters.

Two decisions made in the first ten days determine most of the grade. Reproducing the prototype numbers in the team's own simulator by 25 November either confirms the narrow-headroom reading or reveals a convention mismatch while there is still time to fix it. Tagging the pre-registration before the final seeds turns whichever verdict arrives into a planned result with its evidence already assembled. If the price-directed rule does beat the one-interview rule by the prototype margin, the most interesting question shifts again: whether PPO can rediscover a price on hires that the relaxation computes in 25 seconds.
