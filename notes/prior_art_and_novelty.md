## Key question 1: Is there deep RL / learned-policy work on hiring pipelines with sequential interviews, departures or offer declines, or deadlines (incl. RL for secretary / Pandora / search with recall / recruitment processes)?

### Takeaway
I found no published or preprint work that trains a deep RL (or any learned) policy on the combined setting: Poisson arrivals + noisy costly interviews + quality-dependent departures + time-decaying offer acceptance + multi-role deadline. The closest work splits into two disjoint groups: (a) operations-research / algorithms papers that model large parts of this setting but solve it with DP, LP or approximation schemes and no learning, and (b) RL papers that touch "hiring" or "costly information" but model none of the race-against-departure dynamics.

### Cited Findings

**(a) Closest models, no RL (theory / OR)**
- Gong et al., "A Rolling Recruitment Process Under Applicant Stochastic Departures", Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2025 (DOI 10.1287/msom.2024.1456). Applicants arrive randomly, each is available for a random time; each period the recruiter stops (and chooses how many and which applicants get offers) or waits. Modeled as a large-scale optimal stopping problem; optimal rule per applicant score is a two-threshold policy; the paper "propose[s] methods for computing the optimal policy". Not modeled: interviews / costly information (scores are observed), noisy signals, offer declines as in the team's model, quality-dependent departure. No RL; its reference list contains secretary / prophet papers but no Pandora's box or RL papers. (Abstract + reference list opened; authors beyond first taken from the Consensus record.) — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/msom.2024.1456); [reference list](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/references/10.1287/msom.2024.1456); [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5fcc700f-2a77-475d-a953-6e9f4cdb13d7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- The combined setting (arrivals, noisy costly interviews, quality-dependent poaching, decaying acceptance, m>1 roles, deadline, daily interview capacity) with a deep-RL policy appears to be unoccupied in what I could search. The nearest published model on the "race against departure" dimension is Gong et al. (MSOM 2025), which has arrivals + stochastic departures + multi-offer decisions but observed scores, no interviews, and quality-independent availability as far as the abstract shows; the nearest on the "interview as costly inspection" dimension is Aouad-Ji-Shaposhnik (no time dynamics). Neither uses learning.
- The learning literature on Pandora's box is dominated by regret-style online learning of reservation values under the assumption that Weitzman's index policy is the right benchmark. That is a different research question from training a policy by RL in a variant where the index benchmark is not optimal.
- Segev & Stein (2026) is the most recent extension of the Epstein-Ma / Purohit line and should be cited next to them; it confirms that line is still being developed analytically rather than with RL.

## Key question 2: arXiv 2507.07508 "The Pandora's Box Problem with Sequential Inspections" — model and relevance to multi-stage interviews

### Takeaway
It is directly relevant as the theory reference for two-stage interviewing (cheap partial inspection, then costly full inspection) and as evidence that index policies stop being optimal once inspection has stages; but it has a single selection, no deadline, no arrivals and no departures, and uses no learning.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic: Ali Aouad, Jingwei Ji, Yaron Shaposhnik, "The Pandora's Box Problem with Sequential Inspections", arXiv 2507.07508, v1 10 July 2025, v2 2 April 2026 (cs.CE; econ.GN). — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07508)
- Model: N independent boxes, each with prize V_i and type T_i (dependent within a box). Partial opening costs c_i^P and reveals only the type T_i; full opening costs c_i^F and reveals V_i and T_i. A box may be partially opened and later fully opened. Only a fully opened box can be selected; selecting ends the process, so at most one prize is taken. Objective: selected prize minus opening costs. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- Hiring is the running example: "Pandora represents the company, boxes correspond to candidates"; partial opening = cheaper, less accurate offsite interview; full opening = costlier onsite visit. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- Hardness: "computing optimal policies for PSI is NP-hard, contrary to the standard Pandora's box problem" (reduction from subset sum with two-type boxes). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- Index structure fails: the plain Weitzman policy that ignores partial opening can have an unbounded optimality gap (Example 1), and Example 2 shows "the optimal policy is not indexable". Threshold results still characterize much of the optimal policy: Theorem 1 gives an exact stopping rule (stop iff the best prize so far exceeds the relevant thresholds of all closed and partially open boxes); Theorems 2–3 give sufficient conditions for the next action; these conditions determine the optimal action in over 92% of states on average in the numerical study. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- Approximation: committing policies (fix per box in advance whether it will be partially or fully opened) achieve at least 1 − 1/e of optimum; either a committing policy or its flipped version gives 1/2. Numerically, index-type policies are near 100% of optimum on average and the best committing policy loses at most 7% in the worst case for N=2. — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- Not in the paper: deadlines, arrivals, departures, multiple selections, machine learning or RL (benchmarks include a non-learned one-step lookahead). — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508)
- The 2023 survey attributes the (1−1/e) committing-policy result for partial opening to "Aouad et al. (2020)". — [arXiv 2308.12242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12242)

### Inferences
- "Aouad et al. (2020)" in the survey is very likely an earlier working-paper version of the same project; cite the arXiv 2507.07508 version and note the earlier date if priority matters.
- For the team's Idea 1 this paper is the best precedent for the pattern "index policy is provably not optimal in a hiring-motivated Pandora variant, yet index-type heuristics are numerically close to optimal". It sets an expectation the team should state up front: the gap between index-structured and unrestricted policies may be small in many regimes, and the interesting result is where (which parameter regions) it opens.
- The team's interview is a single noisy inspection (q plus Gaussian noise) preceded by a free resume signal, which resembles "type revealed for free, then one costly noisy look"; it is not the same as P-then-F opening, because the team's interview never reveals q exactly and the offer plays the role of selection with an acceptance risk.

### Gaps
- I read the paper through a summarizing fetch of the PDF, not line by line; theorem numbers and the 92% / 7% figures are as reported by that read and should be re-checked against the PDF before being quoted in the report.

## Key question 3 (Idea 1): learned index policies, index-constrained vs unconstrained comparisons, and theory on when index policies are optimal or fail

### Takeaway
Learning an index with a neural network is well established (restless bandits: NeurWIN, DeepTOP, Neural-Q-Whittle, GINO-Q; Gittins: QGI/DGN; Pandora: learned reservation values), so an index-structured policy network is not new as an architecture. What I did not find is a study that deliberately uses the performance gap between an index-constrained learned policy and an unconstrained learned policy as a diagnostic of where index optimality breaks in a Pandora-type hiring problem with departures; existing comparisons between index learners and unconstrained deep RL are framed as scalability / sample-efficiency contests in which the index learner wins.

### Cited Findings

**Learned index policies**
- Nakhleh, Ganji, Hsieh, Hou, Shakkottai, "NeurWIN: Neural Whittle Index Network For Restless Bandits Via Deep RL" (arXiv 2110.02128; NeurIPS 2021 proceedings): a neural network trained by deep RL to output the Whittle index; evaluated on three restless bandit problems and reported to perform "significantly better than other RL algorithms". (Abstract opened.) — [arXiv 2110.02128](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02128v2); [NeurIPS proceedings](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/0768281a05da9f27df178b5c39a51263-Abstract.html)
- Nakhleh & Hou, "DeepTOP: Deep Threshold-Optimal Policy for MDPs and RMABs" (arXiv 2209.08646; NeurIPS 2022 poster): restricts the policy to threshold form, derives a closed-form policy gradient from the monotone structure, and shows the Whittle index equals the optimal threshold policy of an auxiliary problem; claims "our policy significantly outperforms other reinforcement learning algorithms". (Abstract opened.) — [arXiv 2209.08646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.08646v2); [NeurIPS poster](https://nips.cc/virtual/2022/poster/55434)
- Xiong & Li, "Finite-Time Analysis of Whittle Index based Q-Learning for Restless Multi-Armed Bandits with Neural Network Function Approximation" (arXiv 2310.02147): Neural-Q-Whittle, two-timescale Q-learning of Whittle indices with a two-layer ReLU network, O(1/k^{2/3}) rate. It checks convergence of learned indices to the true Whittle indices and compares with other Whittle learners; it does not compare against an optimal or unconstrained policy. (Opened.) — [arXiv 2310.02147](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.02147v1)
- Chen, Liew, Gunduz, "GINO-Q: Learning an Asymptotically Optimal Index Policy for Restless Multi-armed Bandits" (arXiv 2408.09882): three-timescale algorithm that learns a gain-index policy by decomposing into single-arm subproblems; "GINO-Q does not require RMABs to be indexable"; states that "applying the Whittle index policy to a non-indexable RMAB can result in arbitrarily poor performance outcomes". Baselines include DQN (unconstrained), WIBQ and NeurWIN; performance is judged against relaxation upper bounds rather than the true optimum. (Opened.) — [arXiv 2408.09882](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09882)
- Dhankhar, Mishra, Bodas, "Tabular and Deep Reinforcement Learning for Gittins Index" (arXiv 2405.01157; v4 Aug 2025): QGI (tabular) and DGN (deep, with replay buffer) learn the Gittins index itself via the retirement formulation; applied to job scheduling with unknown service-time distributions. (Abstract opened.) — [arXiv 2405.01157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01157v4)
- Learned Weitzman indices: "Contextual Pandora's Box" maps a context to each alternative's reservation value, and "Online Pandora's Box for Contextual LLM Cascading" directly parametrizes and estimates the contextual reservation index functions. Both learn the index under the assumption that the index policy is the benchmark. — [Consensus record (Atsidakou et al.)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:eb9bad6c-d033-4b39-ae8f-5838d4f6876e/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [arXiv 2606.07392](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07392)
- Xie, Astudillo, Frazier, Scully, Terenin, "Cost-aware Bayesian Optimization via the Pandora's Box Gittins Index" (arXiv 2406.20062; NeurIPS 2024) uses the Pandora's-box Gittins index as an acquisition function. (Search-level for title/venue.) The Scully–Terenin tutorial says of this setting that correlations "completely break the argument" for optimality, while the policy still performs strongly in experiments. — [NeurIPS 2024 page](https://nips.cc/virtual/2024/poster/95339); [arXiv 2506.10872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10872)

**Theory: when index policies are optimal and when they fail**
- Scully & Terenin, "The Gittins Index: A Design Principle for Decision-Making Under Uncertainty" (arXiv 2506.10872, v3 Aug 2025; tutorial). Optimal: Theorem 3.7, "A policy for MCS is optimal if and only if it always selects an action of maximal Gittins index" (requires termination w.p. 1 or discounting with bounded rewards); Theorem 4.2, "The Gittins policy is optimal for undiscounted MCS-k" (finishing k chains, i.e. multiple selections); matroid-type "finish" constraints can also be solved optimally. — [arXiv 2506.10872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10872)
- Same tutorial, failures: "Optional inspection results in a much harder problem than required inspection" and the resulting Gittins policy "is generally not optimal"; "If new Markov chains can appear over time in an arbitrary fashion, then the Gittins policy is no longer optimal for MCS"; with correlated arms the optimality argument "no longer applies"; with multiple servers only approximate optimality results are cited. Restless arms, disappearing arms and deadlines are not analyzed in the part of the tutorial I read; finite horizons fall outside its termination-or-discounting assumption. — [arXiv 2506.10872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10872)
- Non-obligatory inspection: Pandora's rule is not generally optimal; Guha et al. (2008) and Beyhaghi & Kleinberg (2019) bound committing policies (0.8 and 1−1/e respectively as reported by the survey); Doval (2018) shows the optimal policy may be order-adaptive; Fu, Li & Liu (2023) prove NP-hardness and give a PTAS; Beyhaghi & Cai (2023) give a two-phase structure and a PTAS. — [survey arXiv 2308.12242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12242); [Fu-Li-Liu arXiv 2207.09545](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.09545)
- Staged / partial inspection: Kleinberg et al. (2016) show a generalized Pandora's rule is optimal for staged inspection; Ke & Villas-Boas (2019) find the solution complicated even with two boxes; Aouad et al. show non-indexability with partial opening (Key question 2). — [survey arXiv 2308.12242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12242)
- Order constraints and combinatorial selection: Boodaghians, Fusco, Lazos, Leonardi, "Pandora's Box Problem with Order Constraints" (EC 2020): greedy optimal strategies exist for tree-like order constraints; finding approximately optimal adaptive strategies is NP-hard with certain matroid constraints on which boxes may be opened or with DAG reachability constraints. Singla (2018) converts frugal algorithms into policies for combinatorial variants. — [Consensus record (Boodaghians et al.)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:b60d9941-74b3-4e2d-96d2-0df1dd9ae855/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [survey arXiv 2308.12242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12242)
- Commitment / no recall: threshold policies give 1/2 via prophet inequalities (Kleinberg et al. 2016); PTAS / EPTAS for free order (Fu, Li & Xu 2018; Segev & Singla 2021). Correlated values: Chawla et al. (2020) and Gergatsouli & Tzamos (2023) on generalized Weitzman policies against non-adaptive benchmarks. — [survey arXiv 2308.12242](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.12242)
- Fairness-constrained hiring search keeps an index structure but needs dual-adjusted indices and randomization (Aminian et al. 2025). — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:912dcd80-0e64-476e-b919-422ae968541c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Restless bandits: the Whittle index policy is a heuristic that requires indexability and can be arbitrarily poor without it (GINO-Q statement above). — [arXiv 2408.09882](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09882)

### Inferences
- Mapping the team's simulator onto the theory: multiple hires (m=3) alone do not break index optimality (Theorem 4.2 of the tutorial; Singla). The features that plausibly break it are (i) departures: a waiting candidate's state changes while not acted on, which is a restless-bandit feature outside the Gittins/Weitzman framework; (ii) the deadline / finite horizon; (iii) arrivals over time; (iv) the K=3 daily interview capacity (parallel "servers"); (v) the possibility of making an offer without interviewing, which is optional inspection (NP-hard in the static problem); (vi) offer declines that depend on elapsed time, coupling candidates through the shared clock. The report can cite the already-verified "Pandora's Problem with Deadlines" and "Pandora's Box Problem Over Time" papers for (ii) and the tutorial / non-obligatory-inspection papers for (iii) and (v).
- Because departures make the problem restless, theory already predicts that no exact index policy is optimal in general. The team's Idea 1 is therefore best positioned as an empirical measurement of how much is lost and in which regimes, not as a discovery that index structure can fail.
- The team's "index-structured" network takes each candidate's own features plus global clock and roles-left. That is a time- and state-dependent priority rule, a strictly richer class than Weitzman's static reservation value and close in spirit to a Whittle-style index with global Lagrange-multiplier-like inputs. The write-up should say so, and should include a true computed baseline (e.g. Weitzman reservation value on the current posterior, ignoring departure) so that three things can be separated: classical index, learned index-structured policy, unrestricted policy.
- In the RMAB literature the index learner is usually reported to beat unconstrained deep RL (NeurWIN, DeepTOP abstracts), which the authors attribute to exploiting structure. A gap in the other direction (unrestricted beating index-structured) is therefore the informative finding, but it must be distinguished from optimization noise; a small instance solved exactly by DP would make the comparison credible.
- Background knowledge not confirmed from the pages I opened: NeurWIN's network takes a single arm's state as input and outputs that arm's index, which is the same "shared per-item scorer" architecture as the team's index-structured policy. The abstract I read only confirms that the network outputs Whittle indices. Verify in the paper before stating it.

### Gaps
- I did not find a paper that compares an index-constrained learned policy to an unconstrained learned policy specifically to locate where index optimality fails. My search for this was a handful of keyword queries in the restless-bandit and Pandora literatures; I did not search the learning-to-rank literature (per-item vs groupwise scoring functions), Deep Sets / attention-based permutation-equivariant policy work, or learned dispatching rules in scheduling, where the same architectural contrast exists under other names.
- I did not open Singla's "The Price of Information in Combinatorial Optimization", Kleinberg-Waggoner-Weyl (only its abstract, which discusses descending-price auctions and does not mention matroids), Beyhaghi-Kleinberg, Doval, or Boodaghians et al. in full; statements about them rely on the survey and abstracts. The claim that Weitzman-style indices remain optimal for k>1 selections rests on the Scully–Terenin tutorial (Theorem 4.2) and the survey's description of Singla.
- NeurWIN and DeepTOP baselines (which unconstrained algorithms they beat) were not verified beyond the abstract-level claims.
- I found nothing theoretical on Pandora's box with quality-dependent departure hazards (beyond the deadline and "over time" papers the team already has). Absence is supported only by the searches listed.

## Key question 4 (Idea 2): beliefs biased by selective departure / survivorship; any RL or hiring-theory paper that corrects for it?

### Takeaway
The statistical idea is old and standard under other names: "dynamic selection" / duration dependence in labor economics, adverse selection of the remaining pool in search and matching theory, and frailty models in survival analysis. I found no RL paper, and no algorithmic hiring / Pandora / secretary paper, that builds the survival-conditioned posterior into the agent's belief state for a candidate pool; so the correction is not new mathematics, but its use as an explicit belief feature in an RL hiring agent appears to be unclaimed in what I searched.

### Cited Findings
- Jarosch & Pilossoph, "Statistical Discrimination and Duration Dependence in the Job Finding Rate", NBER WP 24200 (2018); Review of Economic Studies 86(4):1631–1665 (2019). Employers "endogenously discriminate against the long term unemployed"; "interview invitations for observationally equivalent workers fall sharply as unemployment duration progresses"; "Discrimination in callbacks is thus largely a response to dynamic selection". This is the closest hiring-theory statement that time-on-market is a rational negative signal of quality. — [NBER](https://nber.org/papers/w24200); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:restud:v:86:y:2019:i:4:p:1631-1665.)
- Board, Meyer-ter-Vehn, Sadzik, "Recruiting Talent" (working paper dated 2 Aug 2020). Firms screen sequentially by wage rank; "lower-ranked firms face an adversely selected applicant pool"; pool quality follows a "sequential screening equation" in which talented workers are removed faster than untalented ones, with recruit quality given by Bayes' rule λ(q,p) = q / (1 − (1 − q)p). A hiring model in which the remaining pool is worse because better candidates were taken by competitors. Equilibrium theory, no RL. — [UCLA PDF](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/sboard/papers/talent.pdf)
- Lauermann & Wolinsky, "Search with Adverse Selection", Econometrica 84(1):243–315 (2016): sequential search with adverse selection; information aggregation can fail because of "a strong form of the winner's curse" in sequential search. — [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2016/01/01/search-adverse-selection)
- An earlier working paper titled "Search and Adverse Selection" (2009) states that in many equilibria "it will be the 'lemons' that stay in the market for a long time, while good types trade fast. This is in accord with stylized facts about the housing or the labor market." (Abstract only; author names not shown in the record.) — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5f9cac18-946b-4e32-8c96-1ef18074fbe1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Kim et al., "Costly search with adverse selection: solicitation curse versus acceleration blessing", RAND Journal of Economics (2017): low-quality sellers search more intensively, which "makes a seller's contact carry negative information but a seller's availability become a stronger indicator of high quality". Shows the sign of the availability inference depends on who exits faster; in the team's model the sign is the opposite because high quality exits faster. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:913a647e-29e9-4b31-aab6-fd4f8fb6e69a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Kaya & Kim, "Trading Dynamics with Private Buyer Signals in the Market for Lemons", Review of Economic Studies (2018): a seller meets buyers sequentially, each with a noisy signal; "buyers' beliefs about the quality of the asset can either increase or decrease over time, depending on the initial level". A formal model of time-on-market belief drift combined with noisy signals. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5c555ea3-8cac-4450-b076-2a0b015d6248/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Vaupel, Manton, Stallard, "The Impact of Heterogeneity in Individual Frailty on the Dynamics of Mortality", Demography 16(3):439–454 (1979) is the standard frailty reference. I confirmed only the bibliographic record (the page I reached was a JSTOR header). — [Berkeley mirror](https://u.demog.berkeley.edu/~jrw/Biblio/Eprints/%20T-V/vaupel.etal.1979.pdf)
- Li, Raymond, Bergman ("Hiring as Exploration", already known) handles a different selection problem, "selective labels": "we only observe hiring outcomes for candidates who were interviewed in reality". This is selection on who is observed by the firm's own choices, not selection by exogenous departure. — [REStud manuscript](https://www.restud.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/MS31512manuscript.pdf)
- Zhao & Wei (2026 review) note that offer acceptance is observed only for "the eligible risk set", i.e. the survey treats risk-set / censoring issues as an evaluation concern in AI recruitment, not as a belief correction inside a sequential policy. — [arXiv 2609.04286](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04286v1)
- Lehrer et al., "Bayesian Sequential Search with Censored Observations", arXiv 2026: censoring of observations restores monotone cutoff rules in Bayesian search. "Censoring" here is about what the searcher observes of draws, not about survival-based selection of the option pool. (Abstract only.) — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:3c3f1062-f420-4e24-ae8a-5144ae05a8bb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Departing bandits (Ben-Porat et al., AAAI 2022) model users who leave when dissatisfied and infer user type from responses; the abstract describes no correction for the fact that remaining users are a selected subset. — [arXiv 2203.13423](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.13423)
- Gong et al. (MSOM 2025) model random availability times for applicants but, per the abstract, do not tie availability to quality or discuss inference from continued availability. — [Consensus record](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:5fcc700f-2a77-475d-a953-6e9f4cdb13d7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- The team's hazard h0·exp(0.6·q) with q ~ N(0,1) is a proportional-hazards model with a log-normal frailty term. Conditioning on survival to age t multiplies the prior (or the signal-updated Gaussian posterior) by the survival function, in discrete time (1 − h0·e^{0.6q})^t and in continuous time exp(−h0·e^{0.6q}·t). That is exactly the frailty-selection calculation in survival analysis and the "dynamic selection" argument in labor economics. The corrected posterior is non-Gaussian (left-skewed tilt), so the team needs either numerical integration, a moment-matched Gaussian, or a small grid; this is an implementation detail worth reporting. (This paragraph is my derivation, not taken from a source.)
- Honest framing: Idea 2 is a correct Bayesian belief state for the simulator, not a new statistical result. The defensible novelty is (a) putting it into the observation / belief features of an RL hiring agent and (b) measuring how much policy value it adds over the naive Gaussian posterior. Cite Jarosch & Pilossoph and Board et al. for the hiring-economics version of the effect, a frailty reference for the mathematics, and Kim et al. / Kaya & Kim to show that the direction of the inference depends on which types exit faster.
- A strong caveat for the experiment: an unrestricted (or even index-structured) network that receives "days since arrival" and the naive posterior mean as inputs can in principle learn the same correction implicitly, since the correction is a deterministic function of those inputs and known constants. The measurable contribution is then sample efficiency or robustness, not asymptotic return. The team should run the ablation "naive posterior + age feature" versus "survival-aware posterior" to avoid overclaiming.
- The agent's own actions also select the pool: candidates who were interviewed and not offered, or who declined, differ systematically from untouched ones. The survival correction covers only exogenous poaching; the write-up should scope the claim to that.

### Gaps
- No RL paper, POMDP paper, or algorithmic hiring / secretary / Pandora paper was found that implements a survival-conditioned posterior over option quality. Searches used the terms informative censoring, survivorship, adverse selection, dynamic selection, quality-dependent departure / abandonment; I did not search the queueing literature on abandonment with heterogeneous (type-dependent) patience, the kidney-exchange literature, or the revenue-management literature on customer patience, where an equivalent belief correction may appear under other terminology.
- I could not confirm the content of Vaupel et al. (1979) from the page reached; the full text of Jarosch & Pilossoph was not opened (the abstract says "dynamic selection" but does not spell out the mechanism); the Lauermann-Wolinsky Econometrica page did not describe the inference mechanism beyond "winner's curse". Classic references I expect to be relevant but did not verify in this session: Lockwood (1991, REStud) on unemployment duration as a signal, Gibbons & Katz (1991) "Layoffs and Lemons", Greenwald (1986), Heckman & Singer (1984) and Lancaster (1979) on unobserved heterogeneity in duration models. These are from memory and need checking before citation.
- I did not check whether the already-known "Pandora's Box Problem Over Time" or "Mortal Multi-Armed Bandits" papers discuss value-dependent expiry; the team has those papers and should confirm that their departure / value-decay processes are independent of the hidden value.

## Key question 5: Hedged novelty wording and what would undercut it

### Takeaway
The team can defensibly claim a new simulator and a new empirical study, with "to our knowledge" qualifiers, but should not claim to be first to learn index policies, first to show index policies fail under departures, or first to notice survivorship in beliefs. Each method idea is a recombination of known pieces applied to an unstudied combined setting.

### Cited Findings
- No deep-RL treatment of the combined setting was found; closest models are analytic (Gong et al. 2025; Segev & Stein 2026; Aouad et al. 2025/2026). — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/msom.2024.1456); [arXiv 2601.12750](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12750); [arXiv 2507.07508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07508)
- Neural index learning is established (NeurWIN, DeepTOP, Neural-Q-Whittle, GINO-Q, QGI/DGN). — [arXiv 2110.02128](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.02128v2); [arXiv 2209.08646](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.08646v2); [arXiv 2310.02147](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.02147v1); [arXiv 2408.09882](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09882); [arXiv 2405.01157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01157v4)
- Index non-optimality in Pandora variants is established theoretically (optional inspection, arrivals, staged inspection non-indexability), and the Whittle index can be arbitrarily poor without indexability. — [arXiv 2506.10872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10872); [arXiv 2207.09545](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.09545); [arXiv 2507.07508](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07508); [arXiv 2408.09882](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.09882)
- Duration / availability as a signal of quality is established in labor and search economics. — [NBER w24200](https://nber.org/papers/w24200); [Board et al.](http://www.econ.ucla.edu/sboard/papers/talent.pdf); [Kim et al.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/w:913a647e-29e9-4b31-aab6-fd4f8fb6e69a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences

**Suggested wording (overall).** "To our knowledge, no prior work trains a reinforcement-learning policy on a hiring problem that combines costly noisy interviews, quality-dependent candidate departure, time-decaying offer acceptance, multiple roles and a deadline. Existing work treats subsets of these features analytically, with dynamic programming, LP-based approximation or approximation schemes (Purohit et al. 2019; Epstein & Ma 2024; Segev & Stein 2026; Gong et al. 2025; Aouad et al. 2025), or studies learning in Pandora's box only as online estimation of Weitzman's reservation values (Atsidakou et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Belloni et al. 2026). Our search covered arXiv and major venue proceedings through November 2026 and was not exhaustive."

**Suggested wording (Idea 1).** "Learning index policies with neural networks is established for restless bandits and Gittins-type problems (Nakhleh et al. 2021; Nakhleh & Hou 2022; Xiong & Li 2023; Chen et al. 2024; Dhankhar et al. 2024), and theory already shows that Weitzman's index rule loses optimality under optional inspection, staged inspection, arrivals and deadlines. Our contribution is empirical: we compare a policy restricted to per-candidate scores with an unrestricted pool-aware policy inside one simulator, and report in which regimes of departure hazard, deadline tightness and interview capacity the restriction costs value. We are not aware of an earlier study that uses this restricted-versus-unrestricted comparison to map where index structure breaks in a hiring or Pandora-type problem with departing options."

**Suggested wording (Idea 2).** "That options remaining on the market are negatively selected when better options leave faster is well known in labor economics (dynamic selection; Jarosch & Pilossoph 2019), in search and matching theory (Board et al. 2020; Lauermann & Wolinsky 2016) and in survival analysis (frailty). We apply this standard Bayesian correction to the agent's belief about each waiting candidate and measure its effect on a learned hiring policy. We did not find prior RL or algorithmic-hiring work that uses a survival-conditioned posterior as the belief state, but the correction itself is not new."

**What would undercut the claims**
1. A paper outside my search reach (workshop, thesis, OR journal without preprint, non-English venue) that trains RL on a hiring simulator with departures. The Gaps under Key question 1 list what was not checked; a forward-citation pass on Purohit et al., Epstein & Ma, Kesselheim et al. and Gong et al. in Google Scholar is the cheapest remaining check.
2. For Idea 1: (a) the index-structured network is not a Weitzman index, since it sees the global clock and roles left, so "testing Weitzman" overstates it; say "index-structured (separable per-candidate priority) policy" and add a computed Weitzman baseline. (b) Theory already implies failure under departures (restless structure), so a finding that "index fails" is expected; the value is in the size and location of the gap. (c) A gap between the two learned policies can come from PPO optimization differences rather than policy-class expressiveness; without an exact DP optimum on a small instance, or matched tuning budgets and multiple seeds, the structural interpretation is weak. (d) In the RMAB literature the structured learner usually wins on sample efficiency, so a null or reversed result is plausible and should be reported as such. (e) Aouad et al. report index-type policies near 100% of optimum on average despite non-indexability, so a small gap would be consistent with prior evidence.
3. For Idea 2: (a) it is a textbook Bayes / frailty calculation; any wording implying a new estimator is wrong. (b) A network given candidate age can learn the correction implicitly, so the benefit may vanish with enough training; the claim should be about sample efficiency or about interpretable belief features unless the ablation shows a persistent gap. (c) The correction assumes the agent knows h0 and the 0.6 coefficient; with a misspecified hazard the "corrected" belief can be worse than the naive one, and that sensitivity should be tested. (d) Selection induced by the agent's own interview and offer decisions is not covered.
4. Citation hygiene: several items above are abstract-only or search-level (flagged in each section); the report should not attribute detailed claims to them without opening the papers.
